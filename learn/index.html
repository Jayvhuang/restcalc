Squeeze Your Glutes and Sleep Better? Oddly Enough, Yes — Progressive Muscle Relaxation

Dingxiang Doctor explains a method whose meme-friendly framing undersells it: progressive muscle relaxation, systematically developed by the physiologist Edmund Jacobson in the early 20th century. Because ordering yourself to "relax" only makes you tenser, you deliberately tense a muscle group first and then release it — using the contrast to learn what tension feels like and how to let it go. The evidence: a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 randomized controlled trials with 2,277 participants found it improves sleep quality and reduces anxiety and other psychological symptoms, and the AASM's 2021 clinical practice guideline lists relaxation training as a behavioral treatment to consider for chronic insomnia in adults. Includes the full 10–15 minute sequence from toes to face and three rules — about 50–60% effort, release abruptly, and never hold your breath.

The Best Diet for Sleep: Maybe You're Simply Not Eating Enough of These

Dingxiang Life Research brings "I can't sleep" back to the most basic question — what did you eat during the day? Three findings, each research-backed: seven days on a low-carb diet delayed entry into REM sleep by 45 minutes; sleep quality peaks at roughly 238g of carbohydrate a day (50–65% of calories), but quality matters — whole grains and tubers versus added sugar and refined grains; and eating more fruit and vegetables (about five cups a day) significantly lowered the sleep fragmentation index, improving sleep quality by about 16%. Includes a concrete daily template you can copy (two bowls of rice + a steamed sweet potato + a corn cob + two fistfuls of fruit) and one hard rule: close the kitchen three hours before bed.

An Underrated Bedtime Habit That May Genuinely Help You Sleep (Not Foot Soaking)

Dingxiang Doctor on slow breathing before bed — you can't order your heart to slow down or your brain to stop thinking, but your breath is something you can deliberately slow. Mechanistically, slow regular breathing drives rhythmic shifts in heart rate and blood pressure, affecting heart rate variability (HRV) and the baroreflex and shifting the body out of high arousal; a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 223 slow-breathing studies found vagally mediated HRV measures generally increased. There is direct sleep evidence too: a 2015 Psychophysiology study found that 20 minutes of paced breathing before bed in people with insomnia shortened sleep-onset latency, reduced night wakings and time awake, and improved sleep efficiency; and a 2026 systematic review of 9 studies with 457 participants supported slow breathing (≤10 breaths per minute) before bed improving self-reported sleep quality. The point is slow, steady and unforced — not one enormous gasp.

There's an Optimal Time to Fall Asleep — Research Points to This Window

Dingxiang Doctor puts two large studies side by side. An Oxford team collected sleep data from 103,712 participants; after excluding unusable records they followed roughly 88,000 people for about 5.7 years and recorded 3,172 cardiovascular events. Published in European Heart Journal – Digital Health in 2021, the analysis found that falling asleep between 22:00 and 23:00 was associated with the lowest cardiovascular disease incidence — and the link held after controlling for sleep duration, regularity and known risk factors. Compared with that window, falling asleep before 22:00 carried 24% higher risk, 23:00–24:00 was 12% higher, and midnight or later was 25% higher. A second study of about 320,000 people in JAMA found 7 hours of sleep associated with the lowest all-cause, cardiovascular and other-cause mortality, with 10+ hours linked to the highest risk. Ends with three practical adjustments.
