Which Exercise Helps You Sleep Best? A Study of 1,348 People Ranked!

Restcalc Sleep Science · Exercise & Sleep

There's little debate that exercise helps you sleep better. In recent years, multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have repeatedly confirmed that people who exercise regularly enjoy higher sleep quality and efficiency, wake less often at night, and fall asleep faster[1–3].

But when it comes to which exercise helps sleep most, most people's intuition is wrong.

1. "Train harder, sleep better" isn't the whole story

Many people default to a single logic — exhaustion: HIIT, long runs, heavy lifting… push your heart rate to the max, drain yourself completely, and fall asleep the moment your head hits the pillow. The motto is "wear yourself out so insomnia can't catch up."

If you happen to be someone who sleeps well after being worn out, keep it up — there's nothing wrong with that.

But research gives a counterintuitive answer: the most sleep-friendly exercise is not the high-intensity kind that wipes you out. It's the kind that sounds like barely any exercise at all —

Mind-body exercise. A few familiar examples: yoga, tai chi, walking.

Don't underestimate these slow movements. They really can help you "move gently by day, and drift off at night."

2. What is "mind-body exercise"?

Mind-body exercise isn't one specific activity. It's a category of movement that brings your attention back to the present moment and focuses on bodily sensation. Three keywords define it:

  • Slow: an unhurried pace, no explosive power;
  • Focused: no distractions, no phone, no rushing to "get it done";
  • Breath-synced: movement paired with breathing, shifting the brain from "fight-or-flight" mode into "rest-and-recover" mode.

Common mind-body exercises include yoga, tai chi, Pilates, walking meditation, and breath-led stretching.

3. What the research says: slow movement really is best for sleep

A 2025 systematic review and network meta-analysis published in BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine[6] pooled 22 high-quality randomized controlled trials covering 1,348 insomnia patients, comparing 13 different sleep interventions — 7 of them exercise-based:

  • Yoga
  • Tai chi
  • Walking or jogging
  • Aerobic + strength training
  • Strength training alone
  • Aerobic exercise combined with psychotherapy
  • Mixed aerobic exercise

The finding: among all exercise types, the slow-paced yoga, tai chi, and walking/jogging delivered the strongest sleep benefits, ranking — yoga > tai chi > walking/jogging.

On some measures, yoga's sleep benefits even approached those of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), the first-line treatment for insomnia. The researchers concluded that these exercises not only improve sleep effectively — they may one day become first-line interventions in their own right.

Top 1 · Yoga: the most comprehensive improvement

Based on sleep-diary data, compared with usual-care control groups, yoga improved multiple sleep metrics:

  • Total sleep time: up by about 111 minutes on average
  • Sleep efficiency: improved by about 15.6%
  • Night awakenings: reduced by about 56 minutes per night
  • Sleep onset: falling asleep about 29 minutes earlier on average

Top 2 · Tai chi: steady results that last

Tai chi also performed well across multiple measures, with some benefits persisting at long-term follow-up:

  • Better sleep quality: improved subjective sleep experience
  • Total sleep time: up by about 52 minutes
  • Night awakening time: reduced by about 36 minutes per night
  • Sleep onset: falling asleep about 25 minutes earlier on average

Top 3 · Walking or jogging: improves your daytime state

While the gains in sleep duration are smaller than the first two, walking or jogging (jogging here means slower than your usual running pace) clearly improves how you feel during the day: it lowers the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI — a lower score means better sleep), easing daytime fatigue and poor concentration.

4. Why does "slow" actually work?

The intensity of mind-body exercises like yoga and tai chi varies a lot with personal skill and practice style. Their sleep benefits don't come from simply "tiring the body out" — there are deeper neural and physiological mechanisms at work.

01 · Regulating the nervous system, helping the brain "switch off"

Take yoga: it significantly improves subjective sleep quality, likely because of its emphasis on body awareness, breath control, and attention training.

Neurophysiology research suggests yoga may boost the activity of the GABA system (a calming neurotransmitter) while dampening overactivation of the amygdala — the brain region that processes fear and anxiety[7, 8].

In other words, yoga eases the anxious and low moods that commonly block sleep, helping the brain shift more smoothly from wakefulness into rest.

02 · Switching the body from "fight mode" to "rest mode"

Take tai chi: a slow, flowing mind-body practice that emphasizes breath control and physical relaxation.

Studies show tai chi enhances parasympathetic nervous system activity and suppresses the sympathetic nervous system, easing the "hyperarousal" state so common under stress and anxiety[9].

Tai chi movements themselves are like a slow-moving meditation — steadier emotions, a cognitive sigh of relief. Even short-term practice may lift sleep quality.

03 · The general sleep benefits of exercise

Take walking and jogging: these improve sleep through multiple pathways:

  • Raising energy expenditure, so the body "wants" sleep more;
  • Lowering cortisol (the stress hormone), helping you relax;
  • Strengthening emotional regulation, reducing pre-sleep irritability;
  • Promoting melatonin secretion, anchoring a regular body clock;
  • Increasing the proportion of deep sleep, so sleep runs deeper.

Stacked together, these physiological and psychological mechanisms improve overall sleep architecture — showing up in studies as significant drops in Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) scores[10].

5. Poor sleeper? Try this "exercise-for-sleep prescription"

The key to mind-body exercise isn't how hard you train — it's whether you actually slow your body and nervous system down. Movement that is slow-paced, moderate in intensity, and synced with your breathing helps the body switch from "daytime mode" into "rest mode."

If you're just starting out

  • ☐ Walk for 15 minutes after dinner — no phone, no earbuds — focusing on the sound of your footsteps and breathing. That's really "walking meditation," a perfect pre-sleep transition.
  • ☐ 10 minutes before bed, do 3 stretches with deep breathing. There are plenty of guided audio/videos to follow (search "bedtime yoga" or "body scan") — slow, focused, eyes closed.

If you enjoy yoga

  • ☐ Try dedicated sleep sequences like "bedtime yoga" or "restorative yoga" — 15–30 minutes per session is genuinely effective.

If you enjoy tai chi

  • ☐ Tai chi and baduanjin lengthen the breath and flow smoothly — ideal for people who wake at night or feel anxious and tense. About 20 minutes per session is enough; rhythm matters, intensity doesn't.
  • ☐ Practiced during the day, it also lifts daytime alertness and emotional stability.

If another form of exercise already helps you sleep, simply keep doing it — the type matters less than the effect.

One caution: it's generally advised to avoid vigorous exercise within 1–2 hours of bedtime, since elevated body temperature and adrenaline can delay sleep onset[12].

Conclusion

The key to better sleep isn't necessarily "training until you drop." For most poor sleepers, 15–30 minutes of slow daily movement — a yoga sequence, a tai chi set, a focused walk — slows the body and mind first, and sleep follows on its own.

May you sleep well tonight.
